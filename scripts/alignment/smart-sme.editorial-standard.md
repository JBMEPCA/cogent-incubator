# Smart SME editorial standard

The reader rules below come from JB's title sheet of 4 September 2026. The
format tiers that follow them come from the competitive analysis of the UK SME
trade press, 2 August 2026.

## Who we write for

Founders, chief executives, managing directors and managers of UK businesses
under 250 staff, in any sector. They are not technical people. They are busy,
they want to understand how AI and modern software can move their business on,
and they want to bring their staff with them. Nobody who works in a business is
turned away for their level: this is the broadest title in the group.

## The busy-owner test

Would a busy, non-technical owner read this and act on it? A £28m energy
storage grant challenge and a comparison of two social media tools' enterprise
pricing both fail: too complex, too niche. If the point needs three paragraphs
of background before it lands, it is not our piece. If a reader would have to
be a specialist to care, it is not our piece.

## The breadth test

Is this a decision most owners face? A niche sub-sector product update (a
manufacturing platform's chat connector, an avatar rendering pipeline, a
hosting company's patents) runs only if the headline is a decision most owners
face. Ask how many owners would search for this, or share it. Being on the wire
does not make it worth writing.

## The never rule

Nothing negative about a named company, and no bad-news pieces: closures,
insolvencies, collapses, firms in trouble. Warnings that protect owners (scams,
cyber threats, rule changes) are fine when the piece is the protection, not the
fear.

## The formats readers love, in order

1. Interviews with real people (SME Leaders). A named owner, or a named
   employee's journey at a named company, in their own words.
2. Topical AI news for owners: what Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot and the tools they
   already use just changed, and what it means for a small firm.
3. "Best of" comparisons with prices: best CRM, best insurance, best software.
4. How-to guides a non-technical owner can follow.

Commission in that order of preference when topics are close on merit.

## The search pillars

The reader types these, in their words: how to use AI in business, AI for
small business, best AI for small business, AI for marketing, AI for finance,
AI for social media, best CRM for small business, and the product names
themselves (Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot). Every pillar gets a definitive guide,
kept current, and the topical AI news links back to it.

## SME Movers

A weekly round-up of appointments and promotions at UK SMEs and at the firms
serving them (tier 2, from the wire). Appointments qualify for the wire even
though no owner changes a decision on them: they are the people news that gets
shared by the subject and puts the title on media lists. Name, role, company,
one line on what they will do.

## AI This Week for Owners

A weekly round-up of the AI launches and changes that matter to a small
business, in plain English, each with the one-line "so what". Tier 2, from the
wire.

## What advertisers want to sit beside

Comparison articles (best CRM, best business insurance, best business energy,
best broadband, best software), how-to guides, and real useful content owners
read. The advertisers seen elsewhere in this market are insurers, energy
suppliers, brokers and software firms, so the comparison lanes to build are
insurance, energy, connectivity, finance and software.

## Beat ownership

Fleet vehicles, vans, the SMMT, EV fleet operations and company car tax are a
sister title's beat. Smart SME covers them only when the angle is the small
business owner, never the fleet operator, and never bids for the same search
term.

## Events and pegs

London Tech Week (June), the Great British Business Show (November), the B2B
Expos (regional, through the year), the Business Revival Series. Cover them
before they happen.

# The formats

Every format sits in exactly one tier, defined by **how much external truth it
needs before it can exist.** An agent must never promote a format up a tier.

| Tier | Needs | Autonomy | Formats |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing but a topic | Full | Evergreen guides, comparisons |
| 2 | A real event on the wire | Full, once the event lands | News rewrites, SME Movers, AI This Week |
| 3 | A real named person who consented | Human in the loop, always | SME Leaders interviews, reaction roundups |

Tier 2 and 3 cannot be manufactured to hit a quota. News and Case Studies are
non-commissionable sections for exactly that reason. The fix for an empty News
section is more supply into the source list, never a relaxed quota.

## Format 1: Evergreen guide (tier 1)

1,200 to 1,800 words, comparison table where tools are compared, FAQ of four
or five real questions, "What to do next" steps. No competitor in the market
has this depth. Keep producing it; just do not lead the homepage with it.

## Format 2: News rewrite (tier 2)

350 to 500 words. Fires when a wire item lands that a UK SME owner would
actually change a decision over, and passes the busy-owner and breadth tests.

- Headline carries two hooks: the action and a number.
- Standfirst: one sentence holding the whole story.
- Lede repeats the standfirst almost verbatim; the second sentence is concrete.
- Paragraphs of two to four sentences.
- The SME angle is the only original element, and it is the reason the piece
  exists.
- Heavy internal linking.

Never invent a quote or a commentator. If no real commentary is available, the
piece runs without it and is shorter.

## Format 3: SME Leaders (tier 3)

A profile of one real UK business owner, or one named employee's journey at a
named company, in their own words. The differentiator: every competitor can
rewrite a press release; none of them is having a real conversation with a
named person. It is also the format that creates commercial relationships.

This format cannot be generated. It requires a real, identifiable person who
has agreed to take part and whose words are their own. No answers means no
piece. An empty slot is correct; an invented one is a serious failure.

The engine shortlists candidates, drafts the questions, writes from the answers
actually returned, and queues the LinkedIn post. A human chooses the subject,
approves the questions, verifies every quote, and confirms the subject has seen
the piece.

Shape: 800 to 1,200 words, named byline. The hook, the business, the turn, in
their words, what other owners can take from it, where to find them (company
name linked to their site).

## Format 4: Reaction roundup (tier 2/3)

One question asked publicly, many answers returned, each contributor credited
with name, job title and company hyperlinked. Real contributors, real words.
The engine drafts the question and lays the piece out; it may not write a
contribution or attribute one to a person who did not send it.

## Standing rules across every format

- Named bylines on everything.
- Never invent a statistic, a source, a quote or a commentator.
- Link out to the company and the original announcement on any news piece.
- No em dashes or en dashes, per house style.
