# Dental Business News editorial standard

Written at launch prep, 18 September 2026, from the dental vertical brief
(docs/vertical-brief-dental.md), and extended on 23 September 2026 from the
title alignment sheet returned by Lucas on 22 September. He contradicted
nothing here: all three of the articles he named as what this title should be
are ours already, and all three sit on the seam below. The sections marked as
his are what the sheet added.

## The owner rule, which outranks everything else here

Every article is written for someone who runs, owns, finances, buys, sells,
supplies or manages a dental practice: principals and owners, practice
managers, group operations and M&A teams, associates planning ownership, and
the brokers, accountants, lawyers, lenders and suppliers around them. If the
natural reader is a patient, it is not our article.

No "how to find an NHS dentist", no treatment price guides for patients, no
oral-health advice, no "is composite bonding worth it", no smile makeovers.
Access-crisis stories enter only as contract economics and capacity: what a
handback, a new contract or a closure means for the practices nearby.

This is a commercial rule. Patient searches outnumber owner searches by two to
three orders of magnitude ("dentist near me" alone runs to six figures a month
in the UK) and the consumer health sites sit at Tranco 1k to 30k. Drifting
patient-side means fighting them on their own ground, permanently.

## The seam: the practice as an asset

Generic practice-management advice puts us head-on with the incumbent trade
title and a wave of AI-written vendor blogs. Our edge is data nobody else
assembles: who owns which practices and groups, what practices sell for, what
NHS contracts are worth and where they are handed back, where practices open
and close, and which are converting to private. Commission toward those
trackers first; an explainer earns its slot when it answers a question an owner
asks before a five- or six-figure decision.

Incumbent copy is never our source. When the only report of a story is another
dental trade title, find the primary source (the regulator, the government
release, the company, Companies House) or do not run it.

## The scope rule

We cover the business of a practice, never clinical practice. No technique,
no treatment choice, no materials recommendations, no outcomes, no "best
composite" or "how to place an implant". The clinical CPD layer belongs to
other titles and we do not enter it. Product launches are covered as
commercial news: price, distribution, who is buying and what it does to a
practice's costs or revenue, never whether it is clinically better.

## The claims rule

Any clinical, whitening or aesthetic claim is attributed to a named source,
never asserted in our voice, and never repeated from marketing copy without a
primary source behind it.

Aesthetics as a business line gets extra care:

- Botulinum toxin and similar injectables are prescription-only medicines. We
  never describe or encourage their promotion to the public, and never imply
  that a non-prescriber may supply them.
- In the UK, tooth whitening products above 0.1% hydrogen peroxide may be
  supplied only by or under registered dental professionals, and illegal
  whitening is a criminal and regulatory matter. We cover the law, the
  enforcement and the business of offering whitening lawfully, never how to
  whiten.
- England's licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures is still
  being implemented. State its status as of the date of the source, attributed.

We cover the revenue, regulation and liability of adding aesthetics to a
practice. Never how-to, never before-and-after.

## The fitness-to-practise rule

GDC fitness-to-practise hearings, CQC enforcement, NHS performer-list actions
and illegal-dentistry stories are legitimate regulatory news and a libel trap,
because the people accused are our own readers.

- Report only published determinations and concluded outcomes, from the
  regulator's or court's own published record.
- Name only what the regulator names, in the form it names it. If the
  published determination anonymises a registrant, so do we.
- No live cases: nothing from a complaint, an investigation, an interim order
  hearing, a referral or an unconcluded hearing, and nothing from social media
  or a local report that precedes a determination.
- Never connect a named practice, group or individual to wrongdoing on the
  strength of a secondary report.
- Policy, statistics and trends (referral volumes, whistleblowing numbers,
  inspection outcomes in aggregate) are always fine and are usually the better
  story.

When in doubt the piece runs without the name, or does not run.

## The figures rule

Market values in this sector differ by scope and firm. LaingBuisson's £12.16bn
(2023/24) is the high-street market; the NHS dental budget, the private-pay
market and practice fee income are different quantities. Name the source and
the year on every figure, quote ranges where firms differ, and never blend
them. UDA values, practice prices, EBITDA multiples and profit per principal
are always attributed and dated. A broker's blog is a named source, not a
market fact.

## The four-nations rule

England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate NHS dental
contracts, and services are inspected by different bodies (CQC, HIW,
Healthcare Improvement Scotland, RQIA). Every contract or inspection piece says
which nation it covers in the first paragraph.

## Events and pegs

Dentistry Show London (ExCeL, 9 to 10 October 2026), the British Dental
Conference and Dentistry Show (NEC, May), BDIA Dental Showcase (ExCeL, March),
the Scottish Dental Show (Glasgow, June), the ADG Conference, the Private
Dentistry Awards and the Dental Industry Awards. Scheduled data drops are
guaranteed stories: NHS Dental Statistics (NHSBSA, annual), NHS dental activity
(monthly), GDC registration reports (January and May), NASDAL benchmarking
(spring), Christie & Co Dental Market Review, LaingBuisson Dentistry UK, the
DDRB pay award, Home Office visa statistics (quarterly), Dental Earnings and
Expenses (summer) and CQC State of Care (October). Award shortlists name
practice owners: each one is an interview target.

Two of those awards belong to competitors. The Dental Awards is The Probe's
and the Private Dentistry Awards and Dental Industry Awards are FMC's. Winners
and shortlists are news and the people on them are interview targets, but we
never promote a competitor's entry deadline, ticket or sponsorship, and we
never carry its branding. The sponsor list is advertiser intelligence: a
company paying to sponsor a dental award is a company with a budget for this
sector.

## People, not practices

The owner's answer to what nobody in this sector covers well, and the same
answer the owners of two other titles in the fleet gave independently:
personal content. Interviews and named people, not trend pieces. His words are
that content where we have actually sat down and spoken to someone is always
more valuable, it shows we work with the sector rather than reporting at it,
and it is what the advertisers he wants will pay to sit beside.

So the named interview is a commissionable format in its own right and not an
occasional treat. Practice owners, group chief executives, trade-body figures,
brokers and the people who did the deal. Every substantial piece carries at
least one named person on the record, from a practice, a group, a trade body
or an adviser. Never invent one, and never soften a real quote into something
the person did not say.

The people rule applies to the picture as well. A story about a person runs
with a photograph of that person.

## Groups, deals and consolidation

The owner named group dentistry news, acquisitions and expansion as the second
thing the advertisers want, and it is the front half of the practice-as-an-asset
seam. This is a standing beat:

- Practices and groups bought, sold, merged and rebranded, with the price and
  the multiple where either is published.
- Who backs whom: the funds, the lenders and the debt behind the groups.
- Groups entering administration, closing sites, handing back NHS contracts or
  leaving a nation.
- New practices opening and practices closing, with the operator and the place.
- Squat practices and expansion plans announced.

The writing rule, because ranking for these is easy and getting the click is
not. Name the group and the place in the first sixty characters, use the plain
words a person would type (buys, sells, opens, closes, hands back), and put
the number of practices in if there is one. What the deal signals for the
market belongs in the standfirst and the body, never in the headline where it
pushes the news out of sight.

## Practice profitability

The owner's account of what the reader worries about, and the thing he says
they will act on: this is an expensive sector and anywhere they can save money
they will. Costs and margins, whether the margins are moving, and whether they
need to put their prices up.

So profitability is a beat, not a theme. Cover what a practice actually spends
and earns: staff costs and the associate percentage, lab bills, materials,
rent and rates, energy, indemnity, software subscriptions, the employer
National Insurance and wage floors, the UDA value against the cost of
delivering it, and the private fee against what the market will carry. Every
figure attributed and dated under the figures rule, and a benchmark named
(NASDAL, Christie & Co, LaingBuisson, the NHS accounts) rather than a round
number in our own voice.

Equipment and technology stories belong here, from the money side. What it
costs, what it replaces, what it saves, how it is financed and what the payback
looks like. Never whether it is clinically better, which is the scope rule.

## The reliability rule

The owner said it twice and it is the one thing he asked for that is not
already written here: in a sector this regulated, everything we run has to be
demonstrably factual, and a story from a source we cannot stand behind is
worse than no story.

- Every news piece names its primary source and links to it.
- A trade title, a broker blog, a vendor blog, an AI-written content farm or a
  LinkedIn post is a lead, never a source. Find the regulator, the government
  release, the company statement, the accounts or Companies House, or do not
  run it.
- Where a figure exists only in a secondary report, say so in the sentence that
  carries it.
- If the only source is anonymous or unverifiable, the story does not run.

## Standing rules

- Named bylines on everything.
- Never invent a statistic, a source, a quote or a commentator.
- No generic listicles. The owner's named example is "10 tips for oral health",
  and the rule is wider than the example: any piece that could have been
  written without a source, a number or a named person is the thing that makes
  a title look automated, and it does not run.
- No consumer dental content in any form. This repeats the owner rule because
  the owner listed it first among the things this title must never touch.
- Link out to the organisation and the original announcement on any news piece.
- No em dashes or en dashes, per house style.

## The headline length rule

Google shows about 60 characters of a headline. Everything past that is cut off
and the reader never sees it.

So the news goes in the first 60 characters: who, what happened, and the number
if there is one. The explainer clause after the colon is written for somebody
already on the page, and nothing load-bearing belongs in it. If the only way to
understand your headline is to read past the colon, the headline is the wrong
way round.

This was measured on 11 September 2026. Median headline length across the fleet
was 89 to 107 characters, so between 99% and 100% of search results were being
truncated mid-clause, and on the longest ones the actual news never appeared at
all. It is the cheapest fixable thing on any of these sites.

Aim for 60 characters before the colon. Sixty-five is the ceiling. A headline
that needs more than that is usually two stories or one unmade decision.
