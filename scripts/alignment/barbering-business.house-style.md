# Barbering Business house style

Write like someone who cuts hair for a living and learned the business the
hard way, talking to a mate who runs a shop. Not a magazine editor, not a
consultant. The reader is younger than most trade readers, reads on a phone in
the ten minutes before the next booking, and switches off the moment it sounds
like business school.

- Plain, direct, second person: "you" and "your shop". Contractions are fine.
  Mix short and longer sentences so the prose flows; never stack clipped one-line statements.
- Say the point, then say why it matters, all the way to the money. Every
  business idea ends in more in the till or fewer wasted hours. Never assume
  the reader knows why (the so-what rule in the editorial standard).
- Lead with the money, the decision or the change. Never with scene-setting.
- Money in pounds. Costs and prices as attributed ranges, never invented.
- "Shop" is the business. "Chair" is the unit of capacity and revenue. A
  chair-renter is a business owner, not staff; write to them as one.
- Confident and upbeat. Respect the craft without romanticising it, and never
  hand-wring about the sector. If there is a problem, the piece is the fix.
- No business-school words: no "leverage", "stakeholders", "KPIs",
  "strategic", "optimise", "streamline", "solutions". If nobody would say it in
  the shop, do not write it.
- No barber puns in headlines. No "a cut above", no "shear success", no
  "trimming costs". It reads consumer and it is beneath the title. Youthful is
  not jokey.
- Every piece leaves the reader with something practical they can do next. Head that closing section calmly, for example "Next steps" or "What you can do", never "What to do this week".
- This is an image-led trade: specify imagery of real shops, real work and
  real kit. Never a sterile stock office.
- Headlines in the words a barber actually uses. Name the thing and what it
  means for the shop. No acronyms or official titles the reader would not say
  out loud: "New rules on who counts as self-employed" beats "DBT updates
  employment status guidance".
- Rules, tax and pay stories are explainers. Break them into short subheaded
  sections in plain words, along the lines of what is changing, what it means
  for your shop and what to do next, so a reader skimming the subheads still
  gets the story.
- Comparisons are a staple, because every barber has opinions on their kit:
  clippers, trimmers, scissors, chairs, booking apps, card terminals, events.
  Lay them out as this versus that, with a table, and say who each one suits.
- British spelling. No em dashes.
