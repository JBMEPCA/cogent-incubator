# Barbering Business editorial standard

Aligned to Tom's title sheet of 29 August 2026. Tom ran Barber Magazine and
knows this trade; where this standard and a general business instinct differ,
this standard wins.

## The owner-frame rule, which outranks everything else here

Every article is written for the person who owns the chair, never the person
sitting in it: barbershop owners and managers, chair-renting self-employed
barbers, and anyone running a male grooming business. If the natural reader is
someone who wants a haircut, it is not our article.

This is a commercial rule, not a taste one. Barbering has the worst consumer
twin of any title in the fleet: single style terms pull six-figure monthly
search volumes ("low taper fade" ~550k/month globally) and "barber near me"
outruns branded search in most UK cities. Drifting consumer means competing
with that volume on its own ground, and losing permanently.

Not our reader, even though they land on the site: consumers reading barbering
as culture (celebrity cuts, cuts tied to a scene), people researching hair
loss, and salon owners on the ladies' hairdressing side. Salon-side stories
(colour, extensions, the big salon groups' education shows) are not ours unless
a barbershop owner would change what they offer because of them.

## The so-what rule

The reader knows barbering and does not know why a business practice matters,
so every business point is spelled out to its end. Chain it, in plain words:
"Take a deposit and you get fewer no-shows. Fewer no-shows means less dead time
in the chair, which means more money per hour, which means more profit. And if
someone does drop out you keep the deposit and can fill the slot." That is
obvious to a business owner and not obvious to a barber. Say it every time,
plainly, and never in a way that talks down to them.

## The positive-turn rule

Never a negative story without the way out in the same piece. "Barbers are not
making a profit" runs only as "and here are five ways to fix that". The fix is
in the headline, not buried at the end. Politics is not ours.

## The consumer-trend rule

Trends are in scope, through the till. "This cut is trending, so offer it and
bookings go up" is ours; "the best styles this summer" is not. Every trend
piece says what to add to the menu, what to charge for it, and how to sell the
upgrade.

## The general-economy rule

Inflation, BCC forecasts, wage rises, business rates, skills shortages: these
run only when the barbershop consequence is the headline and the body. "What
the inflation figure means for your prices" is the piece; the macro story on
its own is not.

## The policy-piece balance

HMRC, NCA and apprenticeship-standard pieces are useful and rank in search, and
the reader will never choose one over a visual, practical piece on the site.
Keep them to roughly one commission in four, give each a "what to do" headline,
and never let them crowd out the money, marketing, kit and trend pieces that
the reader actually clicks.

## The monthly kit round-up

Products & Tools runs a round-up of the month's launches (clippers, trimmers,
shears, styling product, shop kit) with prices and who each one is for. It is
the coverage the small styling brands and tool makers want to sit beside, and
it names them.

## What advertisers want to sit beside

Tips that show why a category of product or service pays back: booking
systems, payment terminals, clippers, styling product, furniture, shop-tech.
Name the real brands (the provider rule in house style) and show the benefit to
the reader. Never "our product is amazing" copy.

## The figures rule

Market sizes in this sector vary by analyst scope. UK men's grooming products
run £1.2bn to £2.3bn for the same year depending on the firm; hair and beauty
services turnover runs £4.6bn to £6.1bn. Name the firm and the year, or quote
the range. Never assert a single figure in our own voice, and never blend the
products line with the services line. Price benchmarks (haircut prices, chair
rents, fit-out costs) are always attributed, dated ranges.

## The crime-coverage rule

The NCA's crackdown on money laundering through barbershops (Operation
Machinize) is a legitimate, recurring policy story and a libel trap whose
victims would be our own readers. We report the policy, the enforcement
statistics and the sector bodies' responses. We never connect a named shop, a
chain, or the nationality of an owner to the laundering narrative unless
reporting a concluded prosecution from a primary source. When in doubt the
piece runs without the name, or does not run.

## Events and pegs

Barber Connect (Telford, late spring), Salon International (London, October),
the Modern Barber Awards, the British Barbering Awards and the Great British
Barber Bash. Award shortlists name shop owners: each one is an In the Chair
interview target.

## Standing rules

- Named bylines on everything.
- Never invent a statistic, a source, a quote or a commentator.
- Link out to the company and the original announcement on any news piece.
- No em dashes or en dashes, per house style.
## The headline length rule

Google shows about 60 characters of a headline. Everything past that is cut off
and the reader never sees it.

So the news goes in the first 60 characters: who, what happened, and the number
if there is one. The explainer clause after the colon is written for somebody
already on the page, and nothing load-bearing belongs in it. If the only way to
understand your headline is to read past the colon, the headline is the wrong
way round.

This was measured on 11 September 2026. Median headline length across the fleet
was 89 to 107 characters, so between 99% and 100% of search results were being
truncated mid-clause, and on the longest ones the actual news never appeared at
all. It is the cheapest fixable thing on any of these sites.

Aim for 60 characters before the colon. Sixty-five is the ceiling. A headline
that needs more than that is usually two stories or one unmade decision.
