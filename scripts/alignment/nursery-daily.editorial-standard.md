# Nursery Daily editorial standard

Drawn from docs/vertical-brief-nurseries.md (18 Sep 2026). No Title Alignment
Sheet has come back for this title yet; when it does, the sheet wins where it
is more specific than this. The safeguarding rule below does not soften for
any sheet.

## The safeguarding rule, which outranks everything else here

We do not cover abuse, injury, death or neglect of children as news. Ever.

- No named children. No named accused individuals. No named individual
  setting in connection with harm to a child.
- Regulatory enforcement is covered only at group or policy level, only from
  an official source (Ofsted, DfE, a gov.uk notice, or a court outcome after
  verdict), and only for its business consequence: what a chain-level welfare
  notice means for other operators, what mandatory CCTV would cost a setting,
  how insurance premiums move, what a change to inspection frequency does to a
  group's compliance budget.
- A single setting's Ofsted grade, suspension or closure after inspection is
  not our story, good or bad.
- Live criminal cases and live prosecutions are off-limits entirely, including
  their business angle, because of contempt-of-court risk. A council-authorised
  prosecution counts as live.
- Any draft that mentions a child's death, abuse, injury or a criminal case is
  held for a human editor before publishing. There is no automatic path to
  publish for it. When in doubt the piece runs without the detail, or does not
  run.

This is the load-bearing rule of the title. The sector's biggest stories are
about harm to children, and an automatically written title that gets one of
them wrong once does damage no quantity of good funding coverage repairs.

## The buyer rule

Every article is written for someone who is paid to run a setting: owner,
director, nursery manager, childminder running it as a business, pre-school
committee chair, out-of-school club operator, group operations or finance
lead, investor, lender, broker or supplier.

If the natural reader is a parent, it is not our article. No "how to choose a
nursery", no "how to claim 30 hours", no "what you can be charged", no
child-development tips, no nursery rankings for parents, no named-nursery
league tables by Ofsted grade. Parents' behaviour enters our pages only as
demand data: occupancy, waiting lists, take-up of funded hours.

Teaching, the EYFS learning goals, activities and child development belong to
Nursery World and the practitioner press. We do not contest them. The EYFS
enters our pages only through its cost and compliance consequences (ratios,
qualifications, safer eating rules, paperwork).

This is a commercial rule, not a taste one. Consumer contamination on this
beat is the worst measured in the fleet: operator intent is about 1% of the
"childcare" searches and about 12% of the "nursery" searches, and most of that
12% is people logging in to software they already use. Drift towards parents
and the title competes with gov.uk, councils and Mumsnet on their own ground.

## The no-children-imagery rule

No AI-generated images of children, ever. No stock photographs of
identifiable children. Use buildings, empty rooms, outdoor spaces with no
children in frame, adults at work, kitchens, equipment and data graphics. One
synthetic toddler in a news context is a reputational event for an automated
title.

## The nation-and-scope figure rule

Funding, ratios, entitlements and regulators differ across England, Scotland,
Wales, Northern Ireland and Ireland. Every figure carries its nation, its
source and its year. England's rates are set per local authority and vary by
around 54% from the lowest to the highest, so a national average is always
labelled as an average and a single council's rate is always named as that
council's. Market-size figures diverge by definition (day nurseries only,
versus all Ofsted-registered childcare): name the firm and the scope, or quote
the range. Never assert a market size in our own voice.

## The politics rule

Funding is a live political fight. Cover it as economics: rates, costs,
margins, capacity, cashflow. No party line and no campaigning voice, including
the sector bodies' own. Report what NDNA, the Early Years Alliance and others
say, attributed, beside what the government says.

## The so-what rule

Every policy change, rate announcement or regulatory update is followed to
the owner's numbers. "The 3 and 4-year-old rate rises 4.95%" is half a story;
the other half is what that does to the margin on a funded place once the
same year's National Living Wage rise is paid, when staff are most of the cost. If a piece
cannot say what the reader should do or check this week, it is not finished.

## Deals and people

Deals are news: buyer, seller, settings, places, region, adviser, date, and
the price when it is published. Never estimate an undisclosed price. The
people who run the groups are the title's interview pipeline, but the
franchise interviews are about running the business, never about children.

## Events and pegs

Childcare and Education Expo National (Coventry, early October) and London
(ExCeL, March), the Nursery Management Show (ExCeL, November), Nursery World's
Big Day Out (May), the NDNA conference, the Nursery World Awards and the
National NMT Nursery Awards. Scheduled data drops are guaranteed articles:
Ofsted childcare providers and inspections (twice a year) and its monthly
management information, the DfE provider survey (December), the funding-rate
announcement (November to December), the Coram Childcare Survey (spring),
Christie and Co's Business Outlook (January) and Day Nurseries Market Review
(summer), the Nursery World Nursery Groups report (July), NDNA's workforce and
closures surveys, NFER's early years workforce report, Savills reports.

## Standing rules

- Named bylines on everything, and a published editorial policy page before
  the first article. Child-welfare adjacency means search engines judge this
  site on trust signals.
- Never invent a statistic, a source, a quote or a commentator.
- Link out to the company and the original announcement on any news piece,
  and to the gov.uk source on any funding or regulatory piece.
- No em dashes or en dashes, per house style.

## The headline length rule

Google shows about 60 characters of a headline. Everything past that is cut
off and the reader never sees it. So the news goes in the first 60 characters:
who, what happened, and the number if there is one. The explainer clause after
the colon is written for somebody already on the page, and nothing
load-bearing belongs in it. Aim for 60 characters before the colon. Sixty-five
is the ceiling.
