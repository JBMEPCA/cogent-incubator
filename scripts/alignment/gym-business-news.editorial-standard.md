# Gym Business News editorial standard

Launch standard, written 18 September 2026 from the vertical brief
(docs/vertical-brief-gym-fitness-spa.md, including its 18 Sep refresh). It will
be revised against the title's alignment sheet once the sector owner has filled
one in; until then this is the standard.

## The scope rule, which outranks everything else here

We cover the business of fitness, never the practice of it. Every article is
written for the person who carries the lease, the payroll or the direct-debit
book: independent gym and studio owners, self-employed personal trainers
running their own business, and franchisees. If the natural reader is someone
who wants to get fitter, it is not our article.

That means, with no exceptions:

- No training advice, no programmes, no exercise technique.
- No nutrition, diet or supplement guidance.
- No weight-loss pharmacology. GLP-1 drugs are a live sector story, and they
  are covered only as a business and liability story: what they do to
  membership demand, what an operator may and may not say or offer, and who
  carries the risk. Never how they work, who should take them or what results
  they bring.
- No treatment efficacy claims for recovery, sauna, cold water, supplements or
  wearables. Recovery is a revenue line we can cover; whether it works is not
  ours to say.

This is a commercial rule as much as a safety one. Almost every term in this
sector has a consumer twin ("gym near me" runs at roughly 100,000 searches a
month) and health advice is YMYL ground a new domain with no named clinician
cannot win. Drifting consumer means competing with the men's-health and
fitness-consumer press on their ground, permanently.

Trends are in scope through the till. "HYROX classes are filling, so here is
what it costs to add a sled track and what to charge" is ours. "The best HYROX
training plan" is not.

## The claims rule

Every health, results, savings or performance claim is attributed to the party
making it and never asserted in our voice. "The supplier says the software cut
failed payments by a third" is fine; "the software cuts failed payments by a
third" is not. That applies to:

- health and results claims ("reduces injury", "members lose weight faster"),
- savings and payback claims from software, equipment and energy suppliers,
- membership, retention and churn statistics from vendors' own content,
- anything from a press release.

Vendor blogs own most of this sector's search results and many of their
statistics have no traceable source. If a figure cannot be traced to a named
report, survey or filing, it does not run, however often it is repeated.

Market figures carry the source and year. The UK Health and Fitness Market
Report (ukactive, with 4GLOBAL, Sport England and Grant Thornton) is the
reference for UK totals; say so every time. US and global figures name their
source (HFA, a listed company's filing, a named analyst) and never get blended
with UK ones.

## The small-operator rule

Our reader is the independent, the studio, the PT and the franchisee, not the
head office. The big chains (PureGym, The Gym Group, David Lloyd, Virgin
Active, Basic-Fit, Anytime Fitness) appear constantly, and every chain story
answers one question for a small operator: what does this change for me? A
chain's results tell an independent what members will now pay and where the
low-cost operators are opening next. The chain's own strategy is context, not
the story.

## The jurisdiction rule

UK first, international second. Tax, employment status, business rates, music
licensing, insurance and consumer law are UK-specific, and every piece on them
says which UK nation it applies to where the rules differ (business rates,
above all, differ between England, Wales and Scotland). International news is
covered as deal, franchise and software news, and as a signal of what reaches
the UK next. Never present a US rule, price or figure as if it applied here.

## The so-what rule

The reader knows training and does not always know why a business practice
matters, so every business point is followed through to the money. "Fix failed
card payments and fewer members lapse without meaning to. Fewer lapses means
more months of revenue per member, which is cheaper than finding a new member
to replace them." Say it plainly, every time, and never in a way that talks
down to them.

## The positive-turn rule

Never a problem without the way out in the same piece. "Independent gyms are
losing members to low-cost chains" runs only as "and here is how the ones that
are holding on do it". The fix is in the headline.

## What advertisers want to sit beside

Buying guides and how-to pieces that show why a category pays back: management
software, access control, payments and direct-debit collection, equipment and
finance, insurance, education. Name the real providers (the provider rule in
house style) and compare them fairly. Never "our product is amazing" copy, and
never a comparison that could only have been written by one of the vendors.

## Events and pegs

Elevate (ExCeL London, June), the ukactive Awards (October), National Fitness
Day (September), the HFA Show (US, spring) and FIBO (Cologne, April). Award
shortlists name independent operators and studio owners: each one is an
interview target.

## Standing rules

- Named bylines on everything.
- Never invent a statistic, a source, a quote or a commentator.
- Link out to the company and the original announcement on any news piece.
- No em dashes or en dashes, per house style.

## The headline length rule

Google shows about 60 characters of a headline. Everything past that is cut off
and the reader never sees it.

So the news goes in the first 60 characters: who, what happened, and the number
if there is one. The explainer clause after the colon is written for somebody
already on the page, and nothing load-bearing belongs in it.

Aim for 60 characters before the colon. Sixty-five is the ceiling. A headline
that needs more than that is usually two stories or one unmade decision.
