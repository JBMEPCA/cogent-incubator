Pricing the tee sheet

Dynamic pricing arrived in golf from hotels and airlines, and it works, but only when the rate rules reflect how a tee sheet actually fills. Our practical guide to dynamic pricing on tee sheets covers the segmentation, the guardrails and the member relations problem that sinks most first attempts.

The frontier is letting demand set the price directly. Golf District Pricepoint lets golfers bid on sold-out tee times, with a ten-minute first right to accept for the operator, which is a different proposition to discounting into a soft window.

Measuring it: the metric that fits a tee sheet

Resorts that report golf through hotel metrics tend to flatter themselves. Revenue per available round, and why hotel RevPAR fails on tee sheets sets out the measure that actually reflects a constrained, perishable inventory, and what it changes about how you read a weak month.

The rate you can hold depends on the demand behind it. Turning 2026 rounds-played data into rates covers how to read the national numbers against your own book rather than as a headline.

The systems underneath

None of this survives contact with a tee sheet that cannot express it. Our buyer guide to golf course management software for resorts covers what to demand of a platform, and Addison Reserve consolidating onto a single platform is a worked example of what consolidation buys and costs.

The pro shop is where a good rate strategy quietly leaks. Lightspeed eight bottlenecks behind slow pro shop checkout, payment reconciliation among them, is worth reading before blaming staffing. Further out, the major hotel groups have rolled out AI search while tee sheets are left behind, and real-time visitor scoring is reaching hotel websites, both of which will reach golf booking before long.

The cost side: maintenance and grounds

Revenue strategy is only half the margin. What superintendents cut first when the maintenance budget tightens is the clearest guide to which savings are real and which simply defer cost into next season. On the labour side, turfgrass programmes are quietly building the next superintendent hiring pipeline, and certification is raising the stakes on retention.

Two cost lines are moving faster than the rest. Water is the first: our file on where golf irrigation restrictions are tightening in 2026 tracks the rules by region. Labour-saving equipment is the second, and robotic mowers taking on British Masters preparation is the most visible test of it so far.
