1. You spent years inside brands running content, email and customer journeys. What did you keep seeing that made you build a studio around the email journey rather than more content?

Content gets you seen, but it's hard to prove what it's worth. I'd watch posts go viral and bring in followers and likes, and sometimes not a single sale. Email is different. You can see what each send brings in, so the return on investment is clear for an owner who wants more revenue. What really drew me in was taking the visibility a brand already has on social media and turning it into customers. With email we can build journeys for a business's specific customers, so people get the right message at each point in their buying journey, which makes them far more likely to buy. And as a business owner myself, I love being able to sit down with a client and show them exactly how much revenue an email made that month.

2. You talk about talented owners who show up online and still do not get consistent customers. Where does it usually break down between someone seeing a post and actually buying?

It breaks down at the nurturing. Most owners put everything into their social media presence and getting eyeballs, but once they've got those eyeballs, there's nothing in place to take someone who has just discovered the business and warm them up to buying. Say a post resonates and someone joins your email list. That's the moment to welcome them, tell them your brand story, introduce your best sellers, and then invite them to shop with an exclusive code or other incentive. Each email moves them a step further, from simply noticing you to becoming a customer. Without that journey, the attention fades and they scroll on. The post did its job, but nothing caught them afterwards.

3. Five Christmas emails made £17,853 for one small brand. What was in those emails, and what would most owners have done differently?

This client is a food business with perishable products, so timing was everything. The five emails launched their Christmas gift boxes alongside their Christmas delivery slots, then kept reminding people what was available and that slots were limited. We also set up an early access waitlist with its own sign-up form, so anyone who joined could book slots a day before everyone else. That built anticipation and rewarded the most engaged customers. Where most owners go wrong is mentioning it once, because they worry about nagging. But when you have a plan and people are joining at different stages, you're not nagging, you're reminding them to buy. Not everyone sees the first email, and plenty who do aren't ready yet.

4. Another client's attributed revenue rose 1,409% in the first month once their flows were fixed. What was broken, and what did fixing it involve?

Honestly, nothing was broken. The owner had good monthly revenue and had been collecting sign-ups on her website for years, but never had the time to do anything with them. She had over 11,000 subscribers who weren't being emailed. Rather than blast everyone at once, we sent a simple "do you still want to hear from us?" email with preference options. Whoever engaged became our priority segment, and campaigns went only to them. That's good inbox practice: it warms the account back up and improves deliverability for every future send. Alongside that, we set up the core automations and optimised her sign-up forms. So it wasn't really a fix, more waking up a list that had been quietly waiting all along.

5. Attributed revenue can flatter email, because a sale might have happened anyway. How do you and your clients judge whether email is genuinely driving the sale?

It's a fair question, and one I hear a lot. We judge it by tracking clicks through to sales. Someone may have browsed the site a few days earlier without buying, but the email is what brought them back and got them to check out. So if we send at 6pm and see a wave of sales between 6pm and 8pm, we can trace the URLs those purchases came from back to that email, and that's the revenue we attribute to it. Anything outside those parameters, like sessions from other channels that day, gets reported separately rather than claimed for email. When you can see the links from an email leading directly to the sales, it's a reliable measure of what that email actually did.

6. For a small business owner with almost no time, which one email flow should they set up this week, and why that one?

A welcome flow, 100%. Even before that, I tell every owner to have a sign-up form on their website, even if they have no time to email anyone yet. Data collected today is far more valuable than wishing you'd started a list when you finally want to use one. Then spend a couple of days building a simple welcome flow. The first email thanks people for signing up and delivers an exclusive discount or incentive. The second tells your story and shows off your best sellers. The third reminds them to use the incentive before it runs out. Once it's set up, it runs on its own for every new subscriber, and it's where many of my clients have seen their best results.
